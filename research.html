Key Findings

  • Orthokinetic and hydrodynamic mechanisms are the dominant acoustic pathways for droplet-aerosol agglomeration.
  • An optimal acoustic-frequency range appears for different droplet pairs, especially around the low-frequency band used in cloud and fog intervention.

Droplet Agglomeration Induced by Acoustic and Turbulent Flows

The acoustic agglomeration model defines collision frequency through an aggregation kernel. Orthokinetic agglomeration describes particle collisions caused by differential oscillatory motion in the sound field, while hydrodynamic agglomeration accounts for wake/refill interaction between droplets. Brownian and turbulent agglomeration further represent thermal diffusion and vortex-driven particle collision in real fluid media.

The figures first define the orthokinetic collision picture and then translate it into a frequency-response rule, showing how droplet-pair interactions vary across fog, cloud, rain, child droplets, and condensation nuclei.

1. Droplet agglomeration induced by acoustic waves

1.1. Orthokinetic agglomeration

The propagation of sound waves instigates vibration of the ambient air. In a polydisperse aerosol, droplets of different sizes respond with different inertia and phase lags, producing relative displacement inside the agglomeration volume. 2. Droplet agglomeration induced by turbulent flows

Under high-energy turbulent flow, particle migration is no longer controlled only by acoustic relative displacement. Brownian diffusion and turbulent collision should be considered together to represent droplet agglomeration in a real fluid environment.

2.1. Brownian agglomeration

Brownian agglomeration describes collision and coalescence caused by irregular thermal motion of particles suspended in a fluid. It becomes important for small particles and condensation nuclei, where diffusion can maintain particle encounters even without strong acoustic relative motion.

The isotope-exchange branch describes dynamic molecular exchange between micro-droplet groups and environmental water vapor. Light molecules tend to remain in the vapor phase, while heavier isotope molecules are preferentially retained by droplets; acoustic forcing accelerates exchange and increases the heavy-isotope content of accumulated water.

Critical SPL Threshold Identification

Experimental process and conditions

The threshold test alternates natural settlement and acoustic-intervention runs. Frequency is scanned from 30 to 280 Hz at 10 Hz intervals, and SPL is adjusted from 70 to 130 dB at 5 dB intervals. Each scenario includes three control or replicate experiments; sample collection lasts more than 180 s after instrument calibration, with room temperature held near 20°C. The complete test matrix contains 840 groups and more than 300,000 droplet samples.

Experimental data processing

For a fixed frequency, the critical SPL is identified when the average droplet size increases by more than 0.1 μm. SPL0 is accepted as the critical value when SPL1 = SPL0 + 5 dB produces a larger size increment and stable agglomeration. A one-way ANOVA at the 0.05 level is then used to identify the DSSR, namely the droplet-size range with a significant acoustic response.

The threshold figures turn repeated experiments into operational design parameters. The critical-SPL plot identifies the sound-pressure level range where droplet growth becomes statistically effective at each acoustic frequency, while the DSSR-width plot shows how broad the sensitive droplet-size range is under different frequencies.

SPH-based modeling extends sediment-water simulation from particle-scale concentration diffusion to rough-bed boundary treatment. The ISPH branch solves a suspended-sediment advection-diffusion equation with hindered settling and turbulent diffusion, while the WCSPH branch uses ghost boundary particles to represent small-scale bed roughness and improve near-bed velocity and shear-stress transfer.

Ghost-based rough-bed boundary

An improved boundary treatment quantitatively characterizes bed roughness based on ghost boundary particles. The velocities of GBPs are explicitly calculated using evolutionary polynomial regression with a multiobjective genetic algorithm, improving the near-bed velocity and shear-stress response.

Generalized bottom boundary condition

A generalized bottom boundary condition accounts for both deposition velocity and equilibrium concentration. It supports simulation of non-equilibrium suspended sediment transport using a generalized sediment transport equation.

Key Findings

Section | Elastic Capsule OWC Hydrodynamic Performance

  • The EC-OWC consists of a closed semi-cylindrical elastic tube fixed to the bed and connected with a modified OWC, providing a new nearshore wave-energy-converter configuration.
  • Wave pressure excites a free bulge wave inside the capsule; physical modeling confirms that the bulge-wave velocity follows one-dimensional longitudinal wave theory.
  • The PTO/air-chamber response is approximately linear under EC-OWC operation, and the ring-opening mode with an opening ratio near 0.76% can substitute the impulse turbine in model tests.
